What will drive Bitcoin prices

  1. The halving cycle

    About every four years the number of new bitcoins created per block is cut in half. Past halvings were followed by large rallies, but the pattern is not guaranteed to repeat.

  2. Spot ETF flows

    US spot bitcoin ETFs, launched in 2024, made bitcoin easy to hold in brokerage accounts; their inflows and outflows now move the price.

  3. Interest rates and liquidity

    Bitcoin has tended to rise when money is cheap and plentiful and to fall when central banks tighten.

  4. Regulation

    Clear rules in large markets support adoption; bans or enforcement actions can trigger sharp sell-offs.

  5. Adoption

    Use by companies, funds and payment networks adds demand for a supply that is capped at 21 million coins.

Is Bitcoin a good investment in 2026?

There is no single answer: it depends on your goals, time horizon and risk tolerance. Bitcoin has a recent yearly volatility of about 46.60%.

Bull case

  • Supply is capped at 21 million coins and new issuance falls at every halving.
  • ETFs and institutional custody have widened access to bitcoin.

Bear case

  • Bitcoin has fallen by more than half several times; deep drawdowns can happen again.
  • Tighter monetary policy has historically hit bitcoin hard.

Risks to keep in mind

  • Prices can fall sharply: in our data since August 2017 the largest one-day move was −38.76% (March 12, 2020), and the deepest fall from a peak was −83.19% (December 16, 2017 to December 15, 2018).
  • Crypto trades around the clock and can move sharply overnight or at weekends.
  • Holding coins on an exchange carries custody risk; self-custody carries the risk of losing access.
  • Regulation differs between countries and can change quickly.
  • The coin produces no income, so returns depend entirely on the price.
  • Statistical forecasts can be wrong; see the accuracy figures below.

This section is general information, not personal investment advice.

How we forecast Bitcoin and how accurate we are

Three statistical models run every day on the full daily price history: ARIMA (short-term price behaviour), trend + mean reversion (the long-run trend and how far the price is from it) and Monte Carlo (10,000 simulated price paths). The consensus is their average.

On average, in a test on 50 past dates, the consensus missed the actual price by 1.60% one day ahead, 3.87% one week ahead and 8.86% 30 days ahead.
